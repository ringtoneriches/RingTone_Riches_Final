halloween-bat-down.webp / halloween-bat-mid.webp / halloween-bat-up.webp
==========================================================================

Supplied by the site owner as generated stills on a plain white background.

What we did to them:

  Background removed by flood-filling from the image border rather than
  keying white globally. The bat wing membranes are translucent and nearly
  white in places, and a global key punches holes straight through them;
  filling only from the edge removes background that touches the border and
  leaves light pixels inside the subject alone.

  The three bat frames were cropped to ONE shared bounding box, not trimmed
  individually. Trimming each to its own edges puts the body in a different
  place in every frame, and the bat then jumps around inside its own wingbeat.

  Graded for moonlight: brightness and warmth pulled back and a cool cast
  added, so they read as animals at night rather than studio cut-outs. Not
  pushed to silhouette, because the wing membrane detail is the whole reason
  a photograph beats a drawing here.

  Spider thread cropped off the top; the page draws its own, so it can be any
  length.

Sizes: spider 340px wide, bats 420px wide. Between 7 KB and 35 KB each.

halloween-cloud-high.webp / halloween-cloud-mid.webp / halloween-cloud-low.webp
================================================================================

Generated here with ImageMagick, not supplied.

These were previously rendered live in the browser with SVG feTurbulence,
which came to roughly five megapixels of four- and five-octave Perlin noise
recomputed as the page ran, plus three 46px blurs over a similar area. That
is what made staging feel sluggish.

The shaping is identical — fractal noise, thresholded into banks, flat dark
tint, faded top and bottom — but computed once, offline, so the browser only
scales a picture and translates it. About 110 KB for all three.

halloween-scene-graveyard.webp
==============================

Supplied by the site owner, generated to a brief: landscape, bare trees
framing both edges, graveyard and fence across the bottom, a house low on the
horizon to one side, and — deliberately — NO moon and no focal point in the
upper middle, so the real moon, bats and spider can sit on top of it and move.

We only resized it to 1920 wide and encoded it as WebP, 94 KB. No grading was
needed: measured across a 3x3 grid it already runs between 13 and 26 out of
255, and is darkest exactly where the headline sits.

halloween-web-corner.webp / halloween-web-torn.webp / halloween-web-drape.webp
===============================================================================

Supplied by the site owner as dew-covered webs shot on pure black.

Keyed by luminance, not cut out. A web cannot be cut out the way the spider
was: the spider is solid black on white, so filling from the border removes
the background and leaves the subject, but a web is pale, fine and
semi-transparent — the background shows THROUGH it and in most pixels the two
are mixed. There is no edge to cut.

Shooting them as white silk on black means each pixel's brightness can become
its alpha instead. Black falls away to nothing, bright silk stays solid, and
every half-lit strand arrives at the opacity it actually had, down to threads
a tenth visible that no cut-out would have kept. The dew comes with it for
free. It is how smoke, fire and webs have always been composited.

Exported at 400px (corners) and 620px (strand), which covers a 2x screen at
the sizes they are drawn. Fine silk does not compress, so there is no point
paying for pixels nobody resolves. 136 KB for all three.

halloween-web-drape.webp is the long strand rotated flat and trimmed to its
content, so the same silk runs horizontally with its dew hanging off the
underside — which is what a web strung under a sign actually looks like. The
original diagonal version is not shipped; nothing used it once the logo moved
to the drape.
